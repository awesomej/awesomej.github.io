Once Upon a Time...

Actually,

  • I like coffee a lot!
  • I like to create things.
  • I like to talk a lot!

Once Upon a Time...

I bought a mocha-pot.

Bought a mocha-pot and went deep on it: how to brew, water temperature, grind size.

Became an expert in mocha-pot coffee. What I actually liked was espresso.

Nothing on the way to mastering the mocha-pot tells you that you might like espresso, or need to know about it. Only that they're different.

and so, off he trundled, a fresh cup of coffee in his hand.

Lesson: on the journey to senior engineer, nothing signposts the other skills you need to get to lead.

Risk management, team topologies, politicking. Nobody tells you they exist, only that the role is different.

I made a ukulele.

Made a ukulele for Alex. He loves the banjo and has two: one he plays, and his grandad's (then his dad's) that inspired him and that he'll probably never play. Same object, different purposes.

Painted an astronaut floating in space; the sound hole is the helmet. UV-reactive stars so it glows on stage. Heat-reactive paint on the bridge so it changes as he holds and plays it.

Partway through I realised it might mean something different to Alex than to me. He might not want to play it.

So I never fitted the fretboard or strings. Told him I'd add them if he wanted. He chose to keep it as art on the wall.

and so, once again, he pootled off into the night, ukulele under his arm.

Happy.

Lesson: as engineers we get caught up in what's correct vs what's right.

Learning that difference is what turns a programmer writing code into an engineer building solutions.

The ends don't always justify the means, but the means should be decided with the end in mind.

I said something stupid!

As a junior, your words often get overlooked, but they're often refreshing too.

Early in a lead role, a junior asked about atomicity of a datastore I was evaluating. I hadn't thought it through enough. That question exposed a blind spot.

I said several stupid things!

The further you progress, the more people look up to you. Your statements start to land as statements of fact.

That changes how you ideate with peers and how they react to your decisions.

You will always say stupid things. They just get challenged less often.

and so, he went on his merry way, toing and froing through life.

Until one day, he caused a multi-million pound production incident.

Mid-career: my team released a bug to production that cost us a multi-million pound contract.

My reaction showed how little I knew about what I was doing.

Except, he didn't...

it just felt like he did.

I decided it couldn't happen again. I started reviewing every PR before it went out and doing every system design for high-risk work.

The team felt less trusted. They stopped reviewing each other's work: why review it twice if Jack's going to anyway?

I was forgetting the value of my team. I did it because I didn't want them blamed, and I wanted to protect us from more mistakes.

A Most Peculiar Incident

and so, he found himself lost, unable to see the path ahead.

It broke me: imposter syndrome, self-doubt, overwork.

I genuinely thought I had to stop being an engineer because I couldn't do it.

It took a few months to get to grips with the guilt and the fear, and to recognise that I'd solved the problem wrong.

Once upon a time...

there was a man who didn't know he liked coffee, was just starting to create things, and he kept putting his foot in his mouth,

and on one unremarkable Tuesday,
his world burned down.

I didn't know I actually preferred espresso yet.

I didn't know that not all instruments are for playing.

And I didn't know that my actions mean even more than my words.

and so, you're probably thinking, what is the point in all of this?

programming != engineering != leadership

Programming: the mocha-pot. Mastering the craft won't show you the skills beyond it.

Engineering: the ukulele. Correct vs right. Decide the means with the end in mind.

Leadership: the stupid things. Your words, and even more your actions, carry weight.

mocha-pot + ukulele + foot-in-mouth = ?

Instead of undermining trust, I should have doubled down on it: pulled the team closer, given them ownership of the mistake, and helped us all grow.

My job wasn't to protect the team. It was to protect the company by growing the team.

The End.
